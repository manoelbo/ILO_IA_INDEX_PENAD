Remote work. Another potential confounder is the post-pandemic normalization of
remote work. Work-from-home arrangements may reduce demand for junior workers
for reasons unrelated to automation, for example by increasing supervision costs and
weakening opportunities for on-the-job learning. This concern is particularly relevant be-
cause occupations that are well suited to remote work overlap substantially with those
most exposed to GenAI (Lambert and Schindler, 2026). To assess this possibility, we
re-estimate our seniority triple-difference specification (Equation 11) separately using
employment in positions with high and low remote suitability, based on the position-
level remote_suitability score in the Revelio individual-positions data.30 The seniority-
biased decline in junior employment at adopting firms appears in both groups and is
similar in magnitude across them. In particular, we continue to find a substantial ju-
nior decline among low-remote-suitability positions, which are unlikely to be performed
from home. These results suggest that our findings are not driven by the post-pandemic
normalization of remote work. Appendix A.24 provides additional details on the data,
methodology, and results, including the corresponding triple-difference estimates in Fig-
ure A.26.


Labor supply shocks.     A final concern is reverse causality—namely, that firms adopting
GenAI were disproportionately affected by negative supply shocks to junior labor, which
prompted adoption. To assess this possibility, we examine a more direct measure of labor
demand: job postings. Since the job postings data do not include seniority information,
this analysis captures total demand for workers rather than its distribution by seniority,
and the job postings data begin only in 2021Q4. Figure A.27, reported in Appendix A.25,
presents the results: starting in 2023Q1, the number of job postings declined significantly
in adopting firms relative to non-adopters. This provides suggestive evidence that the
observed decline in junior employment reflects a contraction in labor demand rather than
a supply-driven shock.
