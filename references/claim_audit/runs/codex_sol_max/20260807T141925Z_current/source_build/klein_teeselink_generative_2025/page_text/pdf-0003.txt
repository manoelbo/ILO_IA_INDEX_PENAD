    Building on these exposure measures, we estimate a series of difference-in-differences
models around the introduction of ChatGPT that exploit firm- and occupation-level
variation in LLM exposure to estimate the causal effects of the ChatGPT shock
on employment, wages, pay dispersion, job seniority, workplace arrangements, and
overall hiring dynamics.
    Our results show a clear impact of LLM introduction on UK labor markets, with
effects emerging gradually across multiple dimensions of employment. At the firm
level, a one standard deviation increase in LLM exposure is associated with a 0.3 per-
cent reduction in total employment. The impact is concentrated among junior po-
sitions, with low-seniority employment declining by 0.4 percent while high-seniority
positions show smaller and partly insignificant reductions. This compositional shift
reduces the fraction of low-seniority workers, which mechanically raises average com-
pensation in exposed companies as the workforce skews toward higher-paid senior
employees.
    These patterns also show up in hiring intentions. In an analysis of firm-level
job posting data, we find that a one standard deviation increase in LLM exposure
reduces the likelihood that firms post job vacancies in a given month by 1.0 per-
centage point. The reduction in job postings among exposed firms is strongest for
technical and creative roles such as software engineers and designers. At the occu-
pation level, we observe even more pronounced effects on hiring. A one standard
deviation increase in an occupation’s LLM exposure is associated with a 3.9 percent
decline in job postings, accompanied by substantial reductions in posted salaries.
    Importantly, these effects are not uniformly distributed across the labor mar-
ket. Our heterogeneity analysis shows that LLM disruption is concentrated almost
entirely in higher-wage segments. High-compensation firms experience employment
reductions of 0.7 percent per standard deviation increase in exposure, compared with
near-zero effects for low-compensation firms. Similarly, high-salary occupations ex-
perience 5.7 percent declines in job postings per standard deviation, while low-salary
roles show no significant change. These results point in the opposite direction from
historical automation experiences, in which routine middle-skill occupations bore
the brunt of technological displacement (Autor, 2024).
