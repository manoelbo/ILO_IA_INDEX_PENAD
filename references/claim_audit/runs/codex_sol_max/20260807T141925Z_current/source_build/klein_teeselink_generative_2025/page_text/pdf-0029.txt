    Several important limitations qualify our findings. First, our identification strat-
egy cannot rule out other shocks that differentially affected high- versus low-exposure
firms or occupations, such as sector-specific demand shifts, regulatory changes, or
complementary technological developments that correlate with LLM susceptibility,
although our robustness checks provide evidence against most of these channels.
Second, the LinkedIn-based employment data likely overrepresents professional and
technical workers who maintain online profiles while underrepresenting manual, ser-
vice, and informal sector employment, potentially inflating our estimates if LLM
impacts concentrate among digitally-active occupations. Third, our exposure mea-
sures from Eloundou et al. (2024) capture potential vulnerability to LLM disruption
rather than actual adoption, which depends on unobserved factors including finan-
cial resources, management quality, technological infrastructure, and organizational
culture. Firms with high exposure scores may resist adoption while nominally less-
exposed firms might implement LLMs aggressively, attenuating our reduced-form
estimates. Finally, our framework cannot observe the creation of entirely new occu-
pational categories enabled by LLM technology such as prompt engineers, AI safety
specialists, or human-AI collaboration designers, which historical evidence suggests
constitutes a primary channel through which technological change generates em-
ployment (Restrepo, 2024).
    These limitations highlight the preliminary nature of our findings and provide po-
tential avenues for future research. Most fundamentally, our 30-month observation
window may capture only short-term labor market disruption rather than long-term
equilibrium outcomes. Previous general-purpose technologies required decades for
complementary innovations, business process reengineering, and workforce retrain-
ing to fully work their way through the labor market. The initial displacement we
document might reverse as organizations develop new LLM-augmented tasks where
humans maintain comparative advantage, or intensify as models become more ca-
pable and adoption barriers fall. In fact, we already see some evidence that the
decrease in hiring in highly exposed occupations attenuates towards the end of the
sample period. Future research should extend the analysis period to distinguish be-
