patterns that predominantly affected routine middle-skill occupations. The concen-

high-salary occupations implies that LLMs may have an equalizing effect on the
income distribution, at least in the short run. This pattern aligns partially with the
optimistic scenario outlined by Autor (2024) who argues that AI could help rebuild

stakes decision-making tasks previously reserved for elite professionals. However, our
findings suggest a more pessimistic mechanism: rather than elevating middle-skill
workers to perform expert tasks, LLMs appear to be eroding demand for the expert
tasks themselves. While this may compress wage inequality by reducing returns to
education and expertise, it appears to achieve this through a leveling-down rather
than the leveling-up process envisioned by Autor (2024).

4.3.2   Geographic Heterogeneity

Geographic concentration provides another lens for understanding differential treat-
ment effects. Table A3 compares firms headquartered in London versus elsewhere
in the UK. Surprisingly, London-based firms show minimal employment responses
despite their concentration in LLM-exposed industries like finance and professional
services. Total employment effects are statistically insignificant (0.1 percent per
standard deviation), with similar null results for both seniority levels. In contrast,
there is some evidence that firms headquartered outside London experience stronger
employment declines from LLM exposure: a one standard deviation increase in LLM
exposure reduces total employment by 0.4 percent (p = 0.042), with low-seniority
positions declining by 0.5 percent (p = 0.020). That said, the firm-level hiring
response shows the opposite pattern: a one standard deviation increase in LLM ex-
posure reduces job postings by 1.6 percentage points for London-based firms versus
0.8 percentage points for firms outside London, despite their more modest employ-
ment stock adjustments.
