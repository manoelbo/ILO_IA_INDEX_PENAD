Evaluating (6) using the midpoint of these bounds, li ≡ (li + li )/2 yields a wage-weighted share

    Our estimated aggregate productivity gain from genAI (1.2%) exceeds the 0.7% estimate by
Acemoglu (2024) based on a similar framework. In our framework and in Acemoglu (2024), the
aggregate productivity gain is the product of three numbers: the AI-exposure-adjusted labor
share of GDP, the wage-weighted share of labor that is supplied by genAI, and the productivity
gain from genAI. We use the same labor share as Acemoglu (2024). Our survey data imply a
wage-weighted genAI adoption share of 7.0%, while Acemoglu (2024) assumes a slightly lower
wage-weighted adoption share of 4.6% based on task-exposure predictions. As discussed above,
we estimate a productivity gain from genAI of 30%; Acemoglu (2024) assumes a productivity
gain from genAI of 27% based on micro experimental evidence. Adjusting our productivity value
γ from 30% to 27% lowers our estimated aggregate productivity gain to 1.1%; the remaining
0.4 percentage point gap is due to our higher adoption estimate.

    We conclude with two points about our estimate of aggregate productivity gains. First,
Acemoglu (2024) assumes these productivity gains take a decade to materialize. In contrast, our
estimates reflect current adoption and time savings, implying a faster pace of diffusion. Second,
our estimate does not capture potential productivity gains from using genAI to reorganize or
completely automate production processes, which could further increase output per worker.
With previous technologies, these more systematic gains occurred with substantial time lags,
implying that this omission may be modest in the short run (Bresnahan and Trajtenberg, 1995;
Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 2000; David, 1990; Jovanovic and Rousseau, 2005).
