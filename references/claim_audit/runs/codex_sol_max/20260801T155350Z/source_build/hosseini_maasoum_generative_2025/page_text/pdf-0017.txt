Discussion. Beyond its primary purpose of clarifying the mechanisms through which
GenAI adoption may differentially affect demand for junior and senior workers, the
framework also helps reconcile two strands of evidence that appear to point in opposite
directions: experimental studies consistently find that GenAI raises productivity most
for less-experienced workers, while labor-market evidence, including ours, links GenAI
adoption and exposure to declines in junior employment. The framework reconciles these
findings by highlighting three margins that are largely outside the scope of experimen-
tal settings. First, experiments typically hold task assignment fixed, whereas junior and
senior workers perform different tasks in practice, often with different exposure levels
to GenAI. Second, experiments measure productivity gains on tasks that remain human-
performed, but do not capture task displacement, whereby some tasks are fully auto-
mated and removed from human labor. Third, higher productivity need not translate into
higher labor demand. In the fixed-output benchmark of the framework, when tasks are
sufficiently complementary, productivity gains can reduce the number of junior workers
required to perform a given set of tasks. More generally, the same force remains relevant
whenever output expansion is not large enough to offset the labor-saving effect of higher
productivity.
    The remainder of the paper takes the framework to the data. Section 5.1 brings direct
evidence on the displacement and reallocation channels—the productivity channel and
the assumption δ J ≥ δS are already well supported by the experimental literature. Then,
Sections 5.2–5.5 focus on the framework’s central implication: how junior and senior em-
ployment within a firm change following GenAI adoption.
