                                               Abstract
            This paper examines the effects of LLMs on UK labor market outcomes.
         We use a difference-in-differences design that compares outcomes across firms
         and occupations from 2021 to 2025 based on their differential exposure to LLM
         capabilities. Our results show that highly exposed firms reduce employment,
         with effects concentrated in junior positions. These firms sharply curtail new
         hiring, with technical and creative roles experiencing the steepest declines.
         At the occupation level, roles more exposed to LLMs also show substantial
         reductions in job listings. The observed effects concentrate almost entirely in
         high-wage segments. Hence, our findings suggest that LLMs may compress
         wage inequality.

       Keywords: Generative AI; Large Language Models; Automation; Labor demand;
United Kingdom.

1       Introduction
The release of ChatGPT in November 2022 marked the beginning of large-scale
diffusion of large language models (LLMs) around the world. Within months, mil-
lions of workers gained access to tools capable of drafting documents, writing code,
analyzing data, and performing countless other cognitive tasks that previously re-
quired human expertise. This rapid integration into workplace workflows has gen-
erated widespread anxiety about the future of work, technological unemployment,
and economic disruption, with some early evidence showing large productivity gains
(Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, 2025; Noy and Zhang, 2023).
    This uncertainty reflects a potential shift in the nature of this technological
innovation. Unlike previous technologies that predominantly displaced routine tasks
in lower- and middle-skilled occupations (Autor, 2015; Autor et al., 2003), LLMs
possess capabilities that extend across the entire skill distribution, with the potential
to affect highly skilled professional and analytical roles as well (Eloundou et al.,
2024). Estimates suggest that around 80% of the U.S. workforce could see at least
10% of their tasks affected, with exposure rising in higher-income occupations on
average (Eloundou et al., 2024). As such, the introduction of generative AI raises
important questions about whether LLMs will erode job security, depress white-
collar wages, and increase or decrease inequality.
    To examine these questions, we use detailed UK labor market data from Reve-
lio Labs, covering millions of firms and job postings between 2021 and 2025. Our
identification strategy exploits the sudden and arguably exogenous introduction of
ChatGPT in November 2022. The impact of this shock varied across firms and
industries depending on their occupational mix and the extent to which core ac-
tivities are susceptible to LLM automation. We measure this heterogeneity using
the occupational level LLM exposure scores developed by Eloundou et al. (2024),
who evaluate 19,265 detailed work activities from the O*NET database using both
human annotators and GPT-4 to determine whether LLM assistance could reduce
task completion time by at least 50% while maintaining quality. These exposure
scores allow us to quantify the share of tasks within each occupation, or the share
of jobs within each firm that can be performed by LLMs.1

    Building on these exposure measures, we estimate a series of difference-in-differences
models around the introduction of ChatGPT that exploit firm- and occupation-level
variation in LLM exposure to estimate the causal effects of the ChatGPT shock
on employment, wages, pay dispersion, job seniority, workplace arrangements, and
overall hiring dynamics.
    Our results show a clear impact of LLM introduction on UK labor markets, with
effects emerging gradually across multiple dimensions of employment. At the firm
level, a one standard deviation increase in LLM exposure is associated with a 0.3 per-
cent reduction in total employment. The impact is concentrated among junior po-
sitions, with low-seniority employment declining by 0.4 percent while high-seniority
positions show smaller and partly insignificant reductions. This compositional shift
reduces the fraction of low-seniority workers, which mechanically raises average com-
pensation in exposed companies as the workforce skews toward higher-paid senior
employees.
    These patterns also show up in hiring intentions. In an analysis of firm-level
job posting data, we find that a one standard deviation increase in LLM exposure
reduces the likelihood that firms post job vacancies in a given month by 1.0 per-
centage point. The reduction in job postings among exposed firms is strongest for
technical and creative roles such as software engineers and designers. At the occu-
pation level, we observe even more pronounced effects on hiring. A one standard
deviation increase in an occupation’s LLM exposure is associated with a 3.9 percent
decline in job postings, accompanied by substantial reductions in posted salaries.
    Importantly, these effects are not uniformly distributed across the labor mar-
ket. Our heterogeneity analysis shows that LLM disruption is concentrated almost
entirely in higher-wage segments. High-compensation firms experience employment
reductions of 0.7 percent per standard deviation increase in exposure, compared with
near-zero effects for low-compensation firms. Similarly, high-salary occupations ex-
perience 5.7 percent declines in job postings per standard deviation, while low-salary
roles show no significant change. These results point in the opposite direction from
historical automation experiences, in which routine middle-skill occupations bore
the brunt of technological displacement (Autor, 2024).
    To interpret these results, we draw on the task-based framework (Autor et al.,
2003; Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2018, 2019, 2020; Autor and Thompson, 2025). This
framework models production as discrete tasks allocated between labor and capi-

                                           3
tal based on comparative advantage. When automation enables capital to perform
tasks previously done by labor more cheaply, it creates a displacement effect that
reduces labor demand. Conversely, the creation of new tasks where labor main-
tains comparative advantage generates a reinstatement effect that increases labor
demand (Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2019; Restrepo, 2024). For LLMs, which can auto-
mate cognitive tasks previously considered non-routine, the framework predicts that
displacement will dominate in the short run, particularly for entry-level positions
involving routine information processing and basic analysis. Senior roles requiring
tacit knowledge, judgment, and complex interpersonal coordination should prove
less vulnerable. This is consistent with our findings.
    Our findings contribute to the emerging international evidence on labor market
impacts of AI (del Rio-Chanona et al., 2025). In the United States, Brynjolfsson,
Chandar and Chen (2025); Lichtinger and Hosseini Maasoum (2025); Dominski and
Lee (2025) document employment declines in exposed industries concentrated in
early-career positions. However, Chandar (2025) finds no employment effects using
CPS data, and Humlum and Vestergaard (2025) report null results from Danish
administrative data. These disparities may reflect several factors: differences in
labor market institutions between countries, variation in the timing of LLM adop-
tion across regions, and methodological choices regarding exposure measurement
and outcome variables. Our UK evidence suggests that effects manifest differently
across adjustment margins, with hiring responding more quickly and strongly than
employment stocks.
    Methodologically, our paper builds on recent work that also uses Revelio Labs
data to construct firm-level AI exposure measures. Eisfeldt et al. (2023) show that
higher-exposure US firms experience excess stock returns following the release of
ChatGPT, while Hampole et al. (2025) develop task-level exposure measures varying
across firms and occupations to study US labor demand between 2010 and 2023. We
extend their work in three directions: we shift focus to the UK labor market, examine
realized employment and job posting outcomes (as opposed to Eisfeldt et al. (2023)’s
focus on financial data), and exploit data through mid-2025 to capture labor market
adjustment to generative AI adoption (as opposed to Hampole et al. (2025)’s focus
on earlier waves of AI).
    In the UK context, we extend Henseke et al. (2025), who develop a Generative
AI Susceptibility Index and provide suggestive evidence that wage premia decline

                                         4
in exposed occupations. While their analysis covers 25 broad occupation groups,
we exploit variation across a much wider variety of job types, as well as a firm-level
analysis that examines within-firm dynamics that would be invisible in occupational
aggregates. In addition, our individual-level posting data enables examination of a
wider set of outcome variables such as salary distributions and seniority-specific
employment, as well as heterogeneity in treatment effects by pre-treatment com-
pensation or location.
    Taken together, our findings raise important policy questions. The dispropor-
tionate elimination of entry-level positions disrupts the conventional model of skill
development, where employees master progressively complex tasks through hands-on
experience with routine work. Without junior roles that serve as training grounds,
firms may struggle to develop future senior talent internally, creating a hollowed-out
labor market where entry becomes increasingly difficult (Ide, 2025). This dynamic
could exacerbate inequality as those with pre-existing experience benefit from re-
duced competition while new entrants face shrinking opportunities. Policy responses
must address both immediate displacement and longer-term skill development path-
ways.


2         Data
Our primary data comes from Revelio Labs, which aggregates and processes publicly
available employment information from professional networking platforms, company
websites, and job boards using machine learning algorithms to create proprietary
longitudinal labor market databases. Revelio Labs transforms semi-structured pub-
lic profile information into a structured panel dataset through systematic parsing,
standardization, and cleaning procedures. Their data have been validated against
official statistics and used in recent academic research on labor market dynamics
(Fadhel et al., 2021; Eisfeldt et al., 2023; Hampole et al., 2025). In Appendix A4,
we separately validate that Revelio Labs employment and job posting data closely
track Office for National Statistics aggregates both over time and across industries.
We use two separate Revelio Labs datasets: work profiles and job postings.2

2.1      Job Profile Data
The first dataset consists of all publicly available professional LinkedIn profiles be-
tween January 2021 through June 2025. The dataset includes approximately 75
million employment spells from 23 million unique individuals across 2.5 million UK-
based firms. Each observation includes the worker’s job title, employer, start and
end dates (if available), seniority level,3 and compensation.4 The data provide
near-real-time employment stocks, which enable us to track within-firm workforce
composition changes over time.
    From the panel of individual LinkedIn profiles, we construct a monthly firm-level
panel from January 2021 through June 2025. For each firm-month, we calculate
total employment as the count of unique individuals with active employment spells
at a certain company in that month, and log-transform this variable. This is our
main employment stock measure. We then separate total employment into junior
and senior employment stocks based on Revelio seniority classifications of jobs. We
also calculate average compensation as the log of the mean of reported salaries plus
bonuses in a firm. We only focus on firms that remained in business throughout the
sample period.
    To calculate firm-level exposure scores, we use the assessment framework devel-
oped by Eloundou et al. (2024). They evaluate 19,265 detailed work activities and
tasks from the O*NET 27.2 database to determine whether LLM assistance could
reduce task completion time by at least 50% while maintaining quality. We focus
on their ‘E1’ measure, which captures exposure to current LLMs, rather than LLMs
combined with additional software. We do so because it is the most appropriate
metric for identifying short-term labor market effects that do not require additional
software development or complex organizational changes. To obtain our firm-level

2.2    Job Listing Data
The second Revelio Labs dataset contains almost the entire universe of online job
postings in the UK over the same period, 63,497,648 in total. The data are collected
from vacancies from major job boards, company career pages, and recruitment plat-
forms. Each posting includes the job title, employer, posting and removal dates,
salary information, and full job ads. This job listing data captures labor demand
flows and allows us to observe how hiring intentions and job characteristics evolve.
We use this dataset for two purposes: to examine how hiring intentions evolve across
exposed and less-exposed firms and across exposed and less-exposed occupations.
    To construct our firm-level hiring intentions data, we use the same sample of
firms with at least 100 employees in October 2022 used in our total employment
analysis, but focus on flows (job listings) instead of employment stocks. We create
a monthly panel at the firm level, tracking job posting activity from January 2021
through June 2025. Given that approximately 50% of firms do not post job listings
in any given month, we construct a binary outcome variable indicating whether a
firm posted any job listings in a given month, rather than using posting counts.
This measure captures the extensive margin of hiring activity. We again match each
firm to its pre-treatment LLM exposure score calculated from the October 2022
workforce composition, as described above. We also break postings down by job
roles (50 in total) to study which types of roles exposed firms hire more or less of.
    This firm-level posting dataset allows us to test whether firms with higher LLM
exposure adjust their hiring intensity differently than less-exposed firms following
ChatGPT’s introduction, providing insights into how anticipated productivity gains
or workforce restructuring needs translate into recruitment behavior. In addition, by
examining the occupational composition of job postings within firms, we can identify
whether highly exposed firms disproportionately reduce hiring for specific types of
roles (for example, those with high LLM exposure scores) relative to less exposed
firms, allowing us to test for within-firm occupational reallocation in response to
LLM adoption.
    To construct our occupation-level hiring intentions data, we first match each va-
cancy to O*NET SOC occupation codes using a job-role-SOC crosswalk provided by
Revelio Labs. This crosswalk maps each unique role (1,500 in total) to 384 broader
categories that directly correspond to the occupations listed in Eloundou et al.

3    Methodology
We use a difference-in-differences design with continuous treatment intensity to esti-
mate the effect of LLM exposure on UK labor market outcomes. Our identification
strategy exploits variation in occupational and firm-level exposure to LLM capabili-
ties, as measured by Eloundou et al. (2024), combined with the timing of ChatGPT’s
public release in November 2022. We conduct three types of analyses: firm-level em-
ployment stocks using LinkedIn profile data, firm-level hiring decisions based on job
posting data, and occupation-level hiring patterns using job posting data.
    For our firm-level employment stock analysis, we estimate the following specifi-

    Our identification strategy relies on the parallel trends assumption: absent Chat-
GPT’s introduction, labor market outcomes would have evolved similarly across
firms and occupations with different LLM exposure levels, conditional on our fixed
effects structure. Several features of our design help with the interpretation of our
estimates as causal. First, our exposure measures are predetermined using data from
before November 2022, eliminating concerns about reverse causality where employ-
ment changes might influence measured exposure. Second, the sudden and largely
unanticipated public release of ChatGPT provides a clear temporal shock to LLM
availability. Third, our event study analyses test and confirm parallel pre-treatment
trends across analyses.
    Furthermore, we perform a large set of robustness checks to test the validity of
our findings across different specifications and modeling choices. We re-estimate our
analyses using different fixed effects. We examine the validity of our finding of de-
creased junior hiring through a highly restrictive within-firm triple-difference model
using a firm-by-month-by-seniority panel that includes firm-by-period fixed effects
absorbing all time-varying firm-level shocks. We test the sensitivity of our results to


                                               12
sample construction by varying minimum firm size thresholds. We consider alterna-
tive task-level weights to construct our exposure measures. We estimate weighted
regressions that give greater weight to larger firms and occupations to ensure our
results reflect typical worker experiences. Finally, we relax the assumption of lin-
ear treatment effects by categorizing firms and occupations into exposure quartiles.
These analyses consistently support our main findings.
    These robustness checks notwithstanding, we acknowledge the possibility that
other factors differentially affected exposed and less exposed firms or occupations
around the time of ChatGPT’s introduction. For instance, if firms anticipating AI
disruption had already begun adjusting their workforce composition before Novem-
ber 2022, or if other technological or economic shocks coincidentally affected high-
exposure entities differently, our estimates could be biased. Additionally, our ex-
posure measures capture potential susceptibility to LLM disruption but may not
perfectly predict actual adoption patterns, which likely vary with firm-specific fac-
tors such as technological capabilities, management quality, and financial resources.


4     Results
This section presents our main empirical findings on the effects of LLM exposure on
UK labor market outcomes. We organize the analysis in three parts. First, we exam-
ine how employment stocks, workforce composition, and compensation evolve within
firms with different levels of LLM exposure. Second, we investigate occupation-level
hiring patterns through job posting data. Third, we explore within-firm recruitment
strategies by combining firm-level exposure measures with their occupation-specific
hiring patterns. Throughout, we present both event study specifications and static
difference-in-differences estimates.

4.3                     Heterogeneous Treatment Effects
Our main results show the average treatment effects across all firms and occupations,
but the impact of LLM exposure is likely to vary with organizational and labor mar-


                                                                                    22
ket characteristics. We examine heterogeneity along two dimensions: compensation
levels (splitting firms and occupations by median pre-treatment salary) and geo-
graphic concentration (comparing London-based versus non-London entities). These
dimensions are highly policy-relevant, as compensation-level heterogeneity provides
a first indication as to whether LLMs may compress or widen wage inequality, while
geographic variation tests whether disruption concentrates in London’s knowledge
economy or extends throughout the UK labor market.

4.3.1   Heterogeneity by Compensation Level

Table A2 examines how firm-level employment effects vary by average compen-
sation, with high-compensation jobs serving as a proxy for more highly educated
white collar work, and low-compensation jobs for relatively low-educated blue collar
work. We split firms at the median pre-treatment compensation level. Panel A
shows that high-compensation firms experience substantially larger employment re-
ductions than their lower-paying counterparts. A one standard deviation increase in
LLM exposure reduces total employment at high-compensation firms by 0.7 percent
(p = 0.009), driven primarily by a 0.8 percent reduction in low-seniority positions
(p = 0.005). In contrast, Panel B indicates that low-compensation firms show no
significant employment effects, with point estimates near zero for both total em-
ployment and low-seniority positions.
    This pattern extends to hiring intentions. Table A4 shows that a one standard
deviation increase in LLM exposure reduces the probability of posting vacancies
by 1.3 percentage points at high-compensation firms (p < 0.001), compared to a
0.7 percentage point reduction at low-compensation firms (p = 0.001). While both
groups curtail hiring, the effect is approximately twice as large at high-compensation
firms.
    The occupation-level analysis in Table A5 provides further evidence that the
employment effects are concentrated among the highly paid. In high-salary occupa-
tions, a one standard deviation increase in LLM exposure is associated with a 5.7
percent reduction in job postings (p = 0.009) and £782 salary declines (p < 0.001)
, while low-salary occupations show no significant changes in posting volumes and
minimal salary effects.
    These results suggest that LLM disruption is almost entirely driven by changes


                                         23
in higher-wage segments of the labor market, contradicting historical automation
patterns that predominantly affected routine middle-skill occupations. The concen-
tration of employment losses and wage pressures in high-compensation firms and
high-salary occupations implies that LLMs may have an equalizing effect on the
income distribution, at least in the short run. This pattern aligns partially with the
optimistic scenario outlined by Autor (2024) who argues that AI could help rebuild
middle-class jobs by enabling workers with foundational expertise to perform higher-
stakes decision-making tasks previously reserved for elite professionals. However, our
findings suggest a more pessimistic mechanism: rather than elevating middle-skill
workers to perform expert tasks, LLMs appear to be eroding demand for the expert
tasks themselves. While this may compress wage inequality by reducing returns to
education and expertise, it appears to achieve this through a leveling-down rather
than the leveling-up process envisioned by Autor (2024).

4.3.2   Geographic Heterogeneity

Geographic concentration provides another lens for understanding differential treat-
ment effects. Table A3 compares firms headquartered in London versus elsewhere
in the UK. Surprisingly, London-based firms show minimal employment responses
despite their concentration in LLM-exposed industries like finance and professional
services. Total employment effects are statistically insignificant (0.1 percent per
standard deviation), with similar null results for both seniority levels. In contrast,
there is some evidence that firms headquartered outside London experience stronger
employment declines from LLM exposure: a one standard deviation increase in LLM
exposure reduces total employment by 0.4 percent (p = 0.042), with low-seniority
positions declining by 0.5 percent (p = 0.020). That said, the firm-level hiring
response shows the opposite pattern: a one standard deviation increase in LLM ex-
posure reduces job postings by 1.6 percentage points for London-based firms versus
0.8 percentage points for firms outside London, despite their more modest employ-
ment stock adjustments.
    At the occupation level, both London and non-London job markets experience
significant disruption. A one standard deviation increase in LLM exposure is associ-
ated with a 3.4 percent reduction in job postings in London (p = 0.047) with salary
declines of £563 (p < 0.001), while positions outside London experience posting


                                         24
reductions of 4.0 percent (p = 0.030) and salary declines of £476 (p < 0.001).


4.4     Robustness Checks
We conduct several robustness tests to assess the sensitivity of our results to alterna-
tive specifications and sample restrictions. First, we examine whether our findings
depend on the fixed effects structure. Tables A7 and A8 present firm-level results
using year-by-month fixed effects instead of industry-by-year-by-month fixed effects,
while Table A9 replicates our occupation-level analysis using job category-by-period
fixed effects. Despite these alternative specifications, all main conclusions persist:
low-seniority employment declines at exposed firms, hiring decreases are, if anything
larger, and occupation-level job postings and salaries fall significantly with exposure.
    Second, we go one step further to address potential endogeneity concerns, and
implement an extremely restrictive within-firm specification. To do so, we con-
struct a firm-by-month-by-seniority panel that measures the number of people at
low, medium, and high seniority levels working in a given firm in a given month.9 We
then estimate a triple-difference model that interacts our post-ChatGPT dummy,
firm-level LLM exposure, and seniority indicators, using the mid-seniority group
as our reference group. This specification includes firm-by-period fixed effects,
which absorb all time-varying firm-level shocks such as changes in profits, financing
costs, industry conditions, management, or any other factor affecting the entire firm
that might be correlated with both LLM exposure and employment decisions. We
additionally include firm-by-seniority fixed effects (controlling for stable workforce
composition preferences) and seniority-by-period fixed effects (capturing aggregate
seniority-level trends). The results in Table A10 show strong evidence that em-
ployment declines concentrate in low-seniority positions: relative to mid-seniority
workers, low-seniority employment at high-exposure firms contracts by an additional
0.7 percent (p < 0.001), while high-seniority positions show no significant differen-
tial effect. This result confirms the finding that LLM adoption disproportionately
affects junior positions (see also Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen, 2025; Lichtinger
and Hosseini Maasoum, 2025).

    Fourth, we verify that our results are not sensitive to the task importance scheme
used in our main specification. Recall that we follow Eloundou et al. (2024) by
assigning weights of 1.0 to core tasks and 0.5 to supplemental tasks when calculating
occupation-level exposure scores. Appendix A3.4 replicates our analyses using the

findings do not depend on which particular task weighting scheme we use.

occupations representing larger shares of employment to make our estimates more
representative for the experiences of the typical worker. Appendix A3.5 presents
the results. The findings are largely in line with our main results. For firm-level
employment stocks, the weighted specification shows similar overall employment ef-
fects, with a slightly larger decline in low-seniority employment (0.6 percent per
standard deviation versus 0.4 percent unweighted). For firm-level job postings, the
weighted coefficient remains negative but becomes statistically insignificant, suggest-
ing smaller firms drive the hiring freeze response. For occupation-level outcomes,
weighted estimates remain nearly identical: job postings decline by 3.8 percent
(versus 3.9 percent unweighted) and posted salaries fall by £474 (versus £495 in
the main specification). The overall consistency between weighted and unweighted
specifications indicates our main conclusions are not driven by extreme outcomes at
small entities, with the possible exception of our firm-level job listing result. Finally,
we relax the assumption of linear treatment effects by categorizing occupations and
firms into exposure quartiles. Tables A20 to A22 present results where the bottom

quartile serves as the reference category. For firm-level job postings, effects are
significant across all higher quartiles, though the third and fourth quartiles show
similar magnitudes, which is consistent with a threshold effect. For firm-level em-
ployment stocks, the evidence is weaker and non-monotonic, with only scattered
significant effects across bins. The occupation-level analysis reveals clearer non-
linearities: while the second and third quartiles show minimal effects, the highest
exposure quartile experiences significantly larger impacts, suggesting that most of
the occupation-level results we observe are driven by the 25 percent most exposed
occupations.


5    Conclusion
This paper provides evidence that large language models are reshaping UK la-
bor markets. Using a difference-in-differences design that exploits variation in
occupational- and firm-level LLM exposure, we conduct three analyses that show
how labor markets change in response to the introduction of generative AI. First,
we examine firm-level employment stocks to understand how workforce size and
composition evolve within organizations. Second, we analyze firm-level hiring pat-
terns to capture forward-looking recruitment decisions that precede employment
adjustments. Third, we investigate occupation-level posting dynamics to identify
economy-wide demand shifts.
    Our firm-level employment analysis documents a 0.3 percent reduction in total
employment per standard deviation increase in LLM exposure, though this effect
only becomes statistically significant approximately 18 months after ChatGPT’s
introduction. This reduction is predominantly concentrated in junior positions,
which decline by 0.4 percent per standard deviation, while senior positions show a
smaller, statistically insignificant 0.2 percent reduction. The resulting compositional
shift reduces the fraction of junior workers by 0.1 percentage point per standard
deviation, mechanically increasing average compensation by £106 as firms retain
proportionally more higher-paid senior staff, though this compensation effect is only
marginally significant.
    While employment stocks adjust gradually, job posting data provide real-time
evidence of firms’ evolving labor demand. Firm-level posting activity drops following


                                          27
ChatGPT’s release, with a one standard deviation increase in LLM exposure reduc-
ing the probability of posting any vacancy by 1.0 percentage point. This reduction
emerges gradually and becomes significant after approximately 10 months, mirroring
the timeline of employment stock adjustments. When we examine the composition
of roles driving this reduction, we find that technical positions and creative roles
experience the sharpest declines. Sales representative positions, by contrast, show
a statistically significant increase, suggesting that customer-facing roles requiring
direct interpersonal interaction remain robust to LLM-based automation.
     In addition to within-firm dynamics, our occupation-level analysis reveals how
labor demand shifts for entire job categories regardless of firm exposure. A one stan-
dard deviation increase in LLM exposure is associated with a 3.9 percent reduction in
posting volumes, accompanied by £495 (1.1 percent) salary declines. The apparent
inconsistency of declining posted salaries for exposed occupations alongside increas-
ing firm-level compensation shows how aggregation could mask opposing forces.
While LLM exposure drives down market wages for specific occupations, within-
firm rebalancing toward more senior positions pushes average firm-level wages in
the opposite direction.
     Our heterogeneity analysis reveals that the observed labor market disruptions
are almost entirely driven by high-remuneration segments of the labor market. This
concentration of effects in higher-wage segments contradicts historical automation
patterns that predominantly displaced routine middle-skill work, and suggests that
LLMs might lead to a compression of the wage distribution.
     Our findings carry important implications for understanding technological dis-
placement and designing policy responses. The concentration of job losses among
entry-level positions disrupts traditional skill development pathways where workers
master progressively complex tasks through hands-on experience. Without junior
roles serving as training grounds, firms may struggle to develop senior talent in-
ternally while new entrants face shrinking opportunities to gain experience (Ide,
2025). The freeze in hiring followed by employment stock changes suggests that
forward-looking indicators like job postings may provide early warning signals for
policymakers. The sharp reductions in technical and analytical roles alongside re-
silience in sales positions indicates that human interaction and relationship-building
skills remain valuable complements to LLM capabilities, suggesting that retrain-
ing programs should emphasize these interpersonal competencies. Policy responses

                                         28
should consider addressing both immediate displacement and longer-term structural
changes through, for example, education reform and alternative skill development
pathways.
    Several important limitations qualify our findings. First, our identification strat-
egy cannot rule out other shocks that differentially affected high- versus low-exposure
firms or occupations, such as sector-specific demand shifts, regulatory changes, or
complementary technological developments that correlate with LLM susceptibility,
although our robustness checks provide evidence against most of these channels.
Second, the LinkedIn-based employment data likely overrepresents professional and
technical workers who maintain online profiles while underrepresenting manual, ser-
vice, and informal sector employment, potentially inflating our estimates if LLM
impacts concentrate among digitally-active occupations. Third, our exposure mea-
sures from Eloundou et al. (2024) capture potential vulnerability to LLM disruption
rather than actual adoption, which depends on unobserved factors including finan-
cial resources, management quality, technological infrastructure, and organizational
culture. Firms with high exposure scores may resist adoption while nominally less-
exposed firms might implement LLMs aggressively, attenuating our reduced-form
estimates. Finally, our framework cannot observe the creation of entirely new occu-
pational categories enabled by LLM technology such as prompt engineers, AI safety
specialists, or human-AI collaboration designers, which historical evidence suggests
constitutes a primary channel through which technological change generates em-
ployment (Restrepo, 2024).
    These limitations highlight the preliminary nature of our findings and provide po-
tential avenues for future research. Most fundamentally, our 30-month observation
window may capture only short-term labor market disruption rather than long-term
equilibrium outcomes. Previous general-purpose technologies required decades for
complementary innovations, business process reengineering, and workforce retrain-
ing to fully work their way through the labor market. The initial displacement we
document might reverse as organizations develop new LLM-augmented tasks where
humans maintain comparative advantage, or intensify as models become more ca-
pable and adoption barriers fall. In fact, we already see some evidence that the
decrease in hiring in highly exposed occupations attenuates towards the end of the
sample period. Future research should extend the analysis period to distinguish be-
tween temporary adjustment and permanent structural change. Additionally, future

                                          29
research could examine heterogeneity in adoption patterns and employment effects
across firm characteristics, investigate whether LLM exposure affects job quality
and working conditions, and examine a wider set of countries.

A4      Representativeness of Revelio Labs Data
A potential concern with using Revelio Labs data is that the coverage of LinkedIn
profiles may not be representative of the broader UK labor market. To investigate
this concern, we compare Revelio Labs employment and job posting data against
official statistics from the Office for National Statistics (ONS). We conduct two sets
of analyses: an aggregate time-series comparison examining whether employment
and vacancy trends in Revelio Labs data track official statistics over time, and
a cross-sectional industry-level comparison examining whether employment shares
across industries are similar in both data sources.
    For the aggregate time-series analysis, we standardize both Revelio Labs and
ONS monthly time series by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard
deviation, creating z-scores that allow direct comparison of trends. We then regress
the standardized ONS employment and vacancy series on their Revelio Labs coun-
terparts. Table A24 presents the results. Column 1 shows that standardized Revelio
Labs employment is a strong predictor of standardized ONS employment. Column
2 shows that standardized Revelio Labs job postings similarly track ONS vacancies.
The correlations indicate that aggregate employment and hiring dynamics in Revelio
Labs data closely mirror official statistics over time.
    For the cross-sectional industry-level analysis, we map Revelio Labs’ RICS in-
dustry classifications to ONS Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) categories.
We aggregate employment across 50 Revelio Labs industry categories into 16 broad
ONS industry groups, then calculate each industry’s share of total employment in
both datasets. Table A26 presents regression results from regressing Revelio Labs
employment shares on ONS employment shares. The coefficient is is again very high,
with 0.706. Figure A1 visualizes this relationship: points cluster tightly around the
45-degree line, confirming that industries with larger employment shares in ONS
data also have correspondingly larger shares in Revelio Labs data. Taken together,
these validation exercises suggest that Revelio Labs data provide a representative
picture of UK labor market dynamics.
